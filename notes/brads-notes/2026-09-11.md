# 2026-09-11

# T-junctions, explained twice

`explain` v2 landed yesterday and left 60 invented `occlusion` detections on
the helmet that a continuous surface does not account for — the T-junction
candidates. Which prompted the obvious question, and it is worth keeping the
answer rather than reconstructing it next time: **what is a T-junction?**

## What it is

A place where, in the image, one edge runs into another and stops, forming a T.
The crossbar is the contour of the nearer surface; the stem is an edge of
something further away, disappearing behind it.

```
      ────────────────────   the crossbar: the near surface's contour
              │
              │             the stem: an edge of something further away,
              │             disappearing behind it
```

**Nothing touches there.** In 3D the two may be metres apart; they meet only in
projection, because the near surface's silhouette cuts across the far one's
edge. Move the camera and the junction slides along the crossbar, or vanishes.
That is the defining property: a T-junction is a property of the *picture*,
while a crease or a mesh edge is a property of the *scene*.

Classically they are the strongest depth cue a line drawing has — in the
Huffman–Clowes labelling the stem says which side of the crossbar is in front,
which is how a drawing of a box reads as solid rather than as seven lines.

## Why it matters here

`corners` finds them, correctly: two fitted segments meeting at an angle, often
both matching real geometry. But ground truth here is a **list of mesh
vertices**, and a T-junction has none, so `match` scores every one as invented.
The detector was not wrong; the truth set cannot represent what it found.

That is what §11 means by *corner precision on a multi-object scene measures
the truth model as much as the detector* — on the cube-with-table scene, half
the invented corners sitting on a real depth step were two real occluding
contours crossing.

Whether ground truth *should* carry them is open, and the difficulty is the
first paragraph: an edge list is the same from every camera and a T-junction
list would not be. Adding them makes the truth set a function of the view,
which is a different kind of object from the one this lab compares against.

## The follow-up, which is the part worth writing down

> *Is a T-junction view-dependent because at a certain orientation the crossbar
> appears as a straight line?*

No — straightness is not what makes it view-dependent. Two things are.

**Where the junction sits, and whether there is one at all.** The junction
exists at the image point where the near surface's contour happens to cross the
far edge. Move the camera and that crossing point slides along the crossbar;
keep moving and the far edge either emerges past the near surface entirely (no
occlusion, no T) or disappears behind it (nothing to terminate, no T). Nothing
in the scene changed — the same two edges are there throughout.

**And on a curved surface, the crossbar itself.** An occluding contour on a
sphere, or on the helmet, is not a fixed set of points on the model: it is
wherever the surface turns away from *this* viewpoint, so it slides across the
object as the camera moves. On a polyhedron the crossbar is a real mesh edge
and stays put — but the junction point on it still moves.

Contrast the stem. It is a real edge, the same edge from every camera. What the
projection fabricates is the *terminating* — the stem does not end there, it is
cut off there. Which is exactly why ground truth can list the stem and cannot
list the T.

## Where this leaves the 60

They are the candidates, not the answer. Nothing has yet shown that any
particular one of them IS a T-junction — only that real geometry steps under
them and no truth vertex pairs with them, which is also what a matcher failure
looks like, and what plane extrapolation getting curvature wrong over 5 px
looks like. Separating those three is the next measurement, and the overlay is
where it should start: a T-junction is obvious to look at and awkward to tally.

# The Helmet Run — a Review

Everything about the helmet run: what gets executed, how, and why, written
from the beginning. It covers three sessions of work — 09-05, when the run was
first broken down, and 09-10, when the tool doing the breaking-down was found
to be wrong and fixed — and it means to use no word that is not explained where
it first appears.

## 1. What the lab is for

cv-lab-2 takes a picture and tries to describe the **straight edges** in it:
where each one starts and ends (to a fraction of a pixel), which way it points,
and how long it is. From those it proposes **corners** — places where two edges
meet. The program that does this — the chain of steps in section 5 — is called
**the detector** below, because it detects edges.

The question the helmet run exists to answer is: **when the detector reports
an edge, is it right?** And when it is not, *why* not?

## 2. Why rendered pictures, and what "ground truth" means

To say whether a reported edge is right, you need to know where the edges
really are, from some source other than the detector. That known answer is
called **ground truth**.

A photograph cannot give you that — nobody knows where the edges of a real
object are in a photo except by marking them by hand. A **rendered** picture
can. A **renderer** is a program that builds a picture from a 3D model: you
give it the object's shape, a room, lights and a camera position, and it
computes what a camera there would see. The one used here is **pt-lab**, a
separate project checked out beside this one. It is a *path tracer*, meaning
it simulates light rays bouncing around the room, which is slow (about 20
seconds a picture) but produces realistic shading and shadows.

Because pt-lab built the picture from the model, it also knows exactly where
every edge of the model landed in the picture. So alongside each picture it can
write out the ground truth: a list of the model's edges and corners in picture
coordinates.

**The model is a mesh.** A 3D model like the helmet is made of thousands of
small flat triangles joined together. A **mesh edge** is a side shared by two
triangles; a **vertex** is a point where triangles meet. Most mesh edges are
invisible in a picture — two nearly-flat triangles side by side look like one
smooth surface. pt-lab only lists an edge in the ground truth if it is one of
three kinds that *could* show up as a line in a picture:

| kind | what it is |
|---|---|
| **silhouette** | the outline — one triangle faces the camera, the other faces away, so the object ends there against whatever is behind it |
| **crease** | a fold — the two triangles meet at a sharp angle (more than 20° here) |
| **boundary** | a rim — the triangles stop here, with nothing on the far side, because the model has an opening or a torn edge at this point |

**Boundary is the one that needs more than a row.** A 3D model is not
necessarily a solid object; it is a *skin* made of triangles, and that skin can
stop. Every other edge in the model has a triangle on both sides of it; a
boundary edge has a triangle on one side and nothing at all on the other. Run
your finger along one and you are tracing a rim, where the surface ends.

This scene has plenty. Of the 9,572 truth edges in the first helmet view,
**1,834 are boundary** (575 of them visible), so the helmet is modelled as an
open shell rather than a sealed solid. A clean example is the floor: it is a
finite rectangle, and 14 boundary edges belong to it — the four sides where the
floor simply stops. Which openings account for the helmet's own 1,834 I have
not checked; drawing only the boundary edges over one picture would show it,
and until that is done, any story about where they are is a guess.

In a picture a boundary edge usually shows up strongly, for the same reason an
outline does: on one side of the line is the object's surface, and on the other
is whatever happens to be behind it. The difference is where the line lives. An
outline is a fact about *this* viewpoint — move the camera and it slides across
the surface — whereas a rim is built into the model and is a rim from every
viewpoint.

A shape modelled as fully closed has no boundary edges at all: a sealed cube or
sphere has a triangle on both sides of every edge, all the way round. So
"boundary" is telling you something about how the object was *built*, not only
about how it looks.

Of the 983 segments the detector reported across the six helmet pictures, 71
were paired with boundary edges, and all 71 of those pairings were real — the
detector does not have trouble with rims. They are simply not many of the
picture.

And it only counts one as **visible** if at least half of it is not hidden
behind something nearer the camera.

**Ground truth models shape and nothing else.** A shadow falling on a surface,
a highlight, or a painted stripe all make real lines in a picture, and none of
them are in the ground truth, because none of them are edges of the shape.
Hold on to that — it is the reason for most of what follows.

## 3. Why the helmet

The first tests used a cube: twelve edges, eight corners, all in known places.
It is easy to check by eye and the detector does well on it. But a cube is not
what the lab will face; a real object is curved, detailed and painted.

The helmet is pt-lab's standard test model: a damaged, painted, detailed
helmet with about 9,600 mesh edges that meet the three kinds above in any one
view. It is the realistic subject.

Before 09-05, four places in this repository said the helmet's edges were
"overwhelmingly paint" — that the detector was mostly finding painted
markings rather than shape. Nobody had measured that; it had been assumed
because the helmet is heavily painted. The run was done to find out.

## 4. Step one — making the pictures

```bash
npm run generate -- --out generated/helmet-256/ --scene helmet --size 256 --truth --aovs
```

This drives pt-lab to render the helmet in a room from **three camera
positions**, spaced a third of a turn apart around it at the same height, each
under **two light levels** (half and full brightness). Six pictures, each 256
pixels square. They are named `p0-l0` (position 0, lighting 0) through
`p2-l1`.

For each one it writes five files:

```
generated/helmet-256/p0-l0.png              the picture itself
generated/helmet-256/p0-l0.gt.json          the ground truth for it
generated/helmet-256/aov/p0-l0-depth.png    how far away each pixel is
generated/helmet-256/aov/p0-l0-normal.png   which way the surface faces at each pixel
generated/helmet-256/aov/p0-l0-albedo.png   the surface's paint colour, with no lighting
```

The last three are **AOVs** — the renderer industry's name ("arbitrary output
variable") for extra images a renderer can produce alongside the picture, each
storing a fact about the scene at every pixel rather than a colour. They are
the other half of the ground truth: the `.gt.json` says *where* the shape's
edges are, and these three say *what is at each pixel* — which is what lets us
later ask why a line is in the picture.

- **depth** — distance from the camera, in metres. A sudden jump in depth
  between neighbouring pixels means one surface ends and another, further
  away, is visible behind it.
- **normal** — the direction the surface faces at that pixel, as an arrow
  pointing straight out of it. A sudden change in direction, with no jump in
  depth, means a fold. (It records direction relative to the *camera*, not to
  the room — something that turned out to matter, and was not written down
  anywhere until 09-10.)
- **albedo** — the paint colour, before any light or shadow is applied. A
  sudden change here, with neither of the others, means a painted line.

The `.gt.json` also carries two numbers the lab needs to read these images
correctly: the depth scale (`maxDepth`, because depth is stored packed into the
three colour channels and needs the scale to turn back into metres) and the
camera's settings, including its **field of view** — how wide an angle it sees,
50° here.

## 5. Step two — running the detector over each picture

```bash
npm run lab -- --script pipelines/explained.lab --as linear \
               --truth generated/helmet-256/ --aovs generated/helmet-256/ \
               --out results/helmet-256/ generated/helmet-256/*.png
```

`npm run lab` runs the lab **headless** — without opening the app's window —
over each picture in turn, executing the same list of commands against each.
That list is the file `pipelines/explained.lab`, written in the lab's own
small command language: one operation per line, the result stored under a
name. Those names are called **slots**; a slot is simply a named container
for one result.

Before the script runs, the runner puts five things into slots for each
picture:

```
A  = the picture, converted to "linear" values (see below)
T  = the ground truth, read from the .gt.json
D  = the depth image
NM = the normal image
AL = the albedo image
```

"**Linear**" needs explaining. The numbers stored in an ordinary picture file
are not proportional to how much light hit the camera; they are adjusted to
match how human eyes perceive brightness (a standard called sRGB). Blurring or
averaging those adjusted numbers gives subtly wrong answers, so the picture is
converted back to numbers proportional to actual light first. The three AOVs
are loaded with no conversion at all, because they are not colours — they are
distances, directions and paint values stored in colour channels.

Then the script, line by line. Everything up to `fit` is the detector.

```
G  = gray(A)                 one brightness value per pixel instead of three colours
B  = gaussian(G, sigma=1.4)  a slight blur, so single noisy pixels do not look like edges
Gx = sobel(B, axis=x)        how fast brightness changes left-to-right at each pixel
Gy = sobel(B, axis=y)        ...and top-to-bottom
M  = sobel(B, axis=mag)      the combined strength of that change — high wherever there is an edge
N  = nms(M, Gx, Gy)          thin each edge down to one pixel wide, keeping only its strongest line
S  = segments(N, Gx, Gy)     grow straight runs of edge pixels, each run given its own number
R  = merge(S)                join runs that line up end to end with a small gap between them
F  = fit(R)                  describe each run as a line segment: two endpoints, angle, length
C  = corners(F)              propose corners where two segments meet or nearly meet
```

The output of `fit` is the list of **detected segments** — the detector's
answer. On the six helmet pictures it produced **983**, about 164 a picture.

The last four lines are not the detector; they are two ways of judging it:

```
EF = explain(F, D, NM, AL, T)   for each segment: what put this line in the picture?
EC = explain(C, D, NM, AL, T)   ...and for each corner
MF = match(F, T)                for each segment: is there a real edge of the shape here?
MC = match(C, T)                ...and for each corner
```

`match` asks the ground-truth question — is the shape really there. `explain`
asks a different one — whatever the answer to that, *why is there a line in the
picture here at all?* Sections 6 and 7 take them in turn. Everything is written
to `results/helmet-256/`: for each picture, a `.features.json` holding every
slot's list of results, and a `.session.json` holding the log of commands that
produced them, so any result can be re-run and checked.

## 6. Step three — scoring against the shape

```bash
npm run score -- results/helmet-256/
```

`match` pairs each detected segment with the ground truth. A detected segment
counts as **matched** — real geometry — if a visible ground-truth edge runs
along it: measured at several points along the segment, the middle value of
those distances is no more than 3 pixels, and the two point within 20° of the
same direction. Every detected segment ends up in one of two groups, and every
visible ground-truth edge in one of two more:

| | means |
|---|---|
| **matched** | detected, and a real edge of the shape is there |
| **invented** | detected, and no edge of the shape is there |
| **found** / **missed** | a visible ground-truth edge that the detector did / did not report |

"Invented" is the ground truth's word, not a verdict on the detector — which
is the whole point of section 7.

`score` totals these over the six pictures. The helmet's result:

```
983 detected    598 matched (61%)    385 invented
```

That 61% is called **precision**: of everything the detector reported, the
share that is real shape. There is a matching number called **recall** — of
everything that is really there and visible, the share the detector found —
and on the helmet it is 4%. That is not a disaster; it is the helmet's mesh:
about 3,000 visible ground-truth edges a picture, most of them tiny, against
about 160 detected segments. Recall is not a useful number on this subject,
which is one reason the cube is still used for that question.

## 7. Why "invented" had to be broken down

385 invented segments is not one kind of thing. Remembering section 2 — ground
truth models shape and nothing else — a line with no shape edge under it could
be:

- a **shadow edge** or **highlight** — a real line in the picture, made by the
  light rather than the object;
- a **painted line** — a real line in the picture, made by the paint;
- a real edge of the shape that `match` failed to pair up;
- or the detector simply being wrong.

The first two are the detector doing its job correctly and being marked down
for it. So before "invented" can say anything about the detector, it needs
splitting by cause. That is what `explain` is for. At seven places along each
segment, it looks at two points 2.5 pixels either side of the line (that
distance is its **offset**), compares the three AOVs there, and applies the
rules below in order, first match winning:

| if, across the line, there is | `explain` calls it | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| a jump in depth (more than 2 cm) | **occlusion** | one surface ends in front of another |
| otherwise, a change of facing direction (more than 20°) | **crease** | a fold |
| otherwise, a change of paint | **texture** | a painted line |
| none of the three | **shading** | made by light: a shadow edge or highlight |

Each comparison is made seven times along the segment and the **median** — the
middle value when the seven are put in order — is used, so that one or two
readings that land on something unrelated cannot decide the answer.

## 8. What the breakdown said on 09-05, and why it was not accepted

```
invented 385:  occlusion 282 (73%) · shading 46 (12%) · texture 32 (8%) · crease 25 (6%)
```

**Texture — paint — was 8%.** The "overwhelmingly paint" claim was simply
wrong, and the four places that said it were corrected. That part stood.

But **73% occlusion** was strange. It said most invented segments sat on real
jumps in depth — real places where one surface ends in front of another — and
yet no ground-truth edge was there. Either there was a problem pairing things
up in `match`, or `explain` was reporting depth jumps that were not really
jumps. The jumps were not borderline: the typical one was about 9 cm, the same
size as under segments that *did* match real edges. So the numbers alone could
not tell the two explanations apart.

The second explanation had a specific suspect. Look at a floor at a low angle:
two points a few pixels apart in the picture can be a metre apart in distance,
though the floor is perfectly continuous. A curved object like the helmet has
that everywhere near its outline, where its surface turns away from the camera.
How far a surface is turned away from facing the camera is called its **slant**
— 0° when it faces you square-on, near 90° when you see it almost edge-on. At
high slant, `explain`'s "more than 2 cm of depth across 5 pixels" rule fires on
a surface that is simply receding, with nothing in front of anything. A cube
could never show this problem, because its faces are flat and it was small in
the frame, which is why nothing measured before 09-10 could have caught it.

## 9. How `explain` was tested, on 09-10

Two tests, both using the six helmet pictures already on disk.

**First, change how far out it looks.** A real depth jump is the same size
however far either side of it you measure — step down off a kerb and it is the
same height whether you measure a centimetre or a metre from the edge. A
receding surface is not: measure twice as far out and you see twice the depth
difference. So `explain` was run again with its offset set to 1, 1.5, 2.5, 4, 6
and 8 pixels, and each segment's depth difference compared between 1 and 8.

- On the 282 segments v1 called occlusion, the difference grew by a factor of
  **8.03** — exactly the factor by which the look distance grew. Receding
  surface, not a jump.
- On the clutter scene's real outlines (a separate scene: a cube on a table
  with a ball), the factor was **1.12**. Real jumps, as expected.
- And the share called occlusion rose steadily with the offset, from 57% at
  1 px to 92% at 8 px. The answer depended on a setting that should not
  matter, which on its own says the measurement was unsound.

**Second, predict what a continuous surface would do.** The normal image says
which way the surface faces at each pixel. If the surface carries on in that
direction without a break, the depth on the far side of the line can be
calculated — imagine a flat sheet touching the surface at that point, tilted
the way it faces, and extended across the line (that sheet is called the
**tangent plane**). Compare the calculated depth with the real one:

- On the receding-surface segments, the flat sheet accounted for **99%** of the
  depth difference. Nothing was left over; there was no jump.
- On real outlines it accounted for **7%**. The rest was a genuine jump the
  sheet could not reach.

That settled it: most of the 73% was `explain` misreading receding surfaces,
not something in the helmet. It also showed two unexpected things. The normal
image records direction relative to the camera, which nothing in this project
had said. And slant does **not** tell the two cases apart — it was about 64° under
both the misread segments and the real outlines, because both live at the
object's outline, where a surface turns away *and* where one surface ends in
front of another. So the fix could not be "ignore high slant".

## 10. The fix — `explain` version 2

v2 no longer asks "is there more than 2 cm of depth difference across the
line?" It asks: **"is there more than 2 cm of depth difference that a
continuous surface cannot account for?"** For each side of the line it builds
the flat sheet from section 9, extends it to the other side, and measures how
far the real depth there is from where the sheet says it should be. Only that
leftover counts as evidence of a jump. It records four numbers per segment so
any answer can be checked: the depth difference measured, how much of it the
sheet accounted for, the leftover, and the slant.

It needs the camera's field of view to do this — to know what direction each
pixel looks in — and takes it from the `.gt.json`, the same place it already
got the depth scale. Without it, it refuses to run rather than quietly giving
the old answer.

Operations in this lab carry a **version number** that is written into every
log, so a result produced by v1 can never be mistaken for one produced by v2.

Re-run on the same six pictures:

```
invented segments    v1              v2
occlusion           282  (73%)       88  (23%)
shading              46  (12%)      218  (57%)
texture              32   (8%)       52  (14%)
crease               25   (6%)       27   (7%)
```

The detector's own numbers did not move — 983 detected, 598 matched, 385
invented — because none of this touches the detector. What changed is the
account of *why* the 385 are there. Most turn out to be shading and paint:
real lines in the picture that ground truth cannot contain. And none of the
199 segments already shown to sit on a real depth jump lost their occlusion
label — the fix removed the misreadings without removing the real ones.

## 11. So, in one paragraph

The helmet run checks the detector against a subject where the right answer is
known. It found the detector's output is mostly real — 61% on real shape, and
most of the rest real lines made by light or paint. The large "occlusion" group
that looked like the next thing to investigate was a fault in `explain`, the
tool used to sort the rejects, not in the detector or the scene; testing that
tool against itself found the fault, and v2 fixed it. What this says about the
detector is modest and good. What it mostly did was correct the measuring tool
and the documentation.

## 12. What is still open

- **88 invented segments still come back as occlusion.** A continuous surface
  does not explain their depth jump, and no ground-truth edge pairs with them.
  They could be T-junctions (see the first section of today's note: two real
  edges crossing in the picture where the model has no vertex), a segment
  `match` failed to pair, or the flat-sheet prediction going wrong on a
  strongly curved patch. Nothing yet separates those. The overlay picture
  (`npm run overlay`) is where to start: it draws the ground truth and the
  detections over the photograph, and a T-junction is easy to see and hard
  to count.
- **Whether a ground-truth edge counts as "visible" depends on the picture
  size.** pt-lab decides visibility using a depth image at the same resolution
  as the picture, and a coarser one hides less. The same scene and camera
  reports about 3,000 visible edges at 256 px and 2,600 at 512 px, so recall
  cannot be compared across sizes. Written down; not fixed.
- ~~**`results/helmet-256/` on disk is still the v1 run.**~~ Re-run on 09-12,
  along with `results/helmet-512/`: both now record `explain` version 2 in
  their logs, and 256 px reproduces section 10's numbers exactly — 88
  occlusion, 218 shading, 52 texture, 27 crease. At 512 px the same correction
  gives occlusion 974 → 116 and shading 358 → 1111, a larger swing than at 256,
  which is unexplained and not the same question as the resolution one above.
  `results/clutter/` was re-run too: its ten invented occlusions become one,
  and its invented corners sitting on a depth step fall from 37% to 24%.
  `results/cube/` and `results/cube1/` have no v2 to move to — those scenes
  were rendered without the AOV passes, so `explain` never ran on them.

  Note what re-running costs: the v1 results are no longer on disk to compare
  against. They are reproducible — check out `3cf5875^` and run the same
  command — but nothing in `results/` now shows what the fix changed.
